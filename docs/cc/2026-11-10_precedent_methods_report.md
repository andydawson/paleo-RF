# How published papers compute the albedo change between land-cover classes from cover fractions, and how the project's diagnostic compares

Prepared 10 November 2026 for the paleo-RF project. Sections 1 and 2 report only what the papers say, with locators. Section 3 is my own assessment and is kept separate. Section 4 lists what I could not read.

Page numbers: Lejeune and Boisier use journal page numbers. Li and Duveiller use the page of the article PDF (Li 1 to 8, Duveiller 1 to 12).

---

## 1. The answer in brief

1. **All four papers report the same kind of quantity: the albedo difference between two pure classes (a complete transition), in plain albedo units (a fraction between 0 and 1, often shown multiplied by 100).** None reports a change for a 10-point shift in cover. Where a paper deals with a partial change, it in effect treats albedo as a straight-line function of cover fraction on the albedo scale: either it divides the albedo change by the fraction converted (Boisier, equation 2) or it multiplies the full-transition value by the fraction converted (Lejeune, equations 8 and 11; Duveiller, pages 5 and 10).
2. **Two of the four papers estimate the quantity by regressing albedo on cover fractions** (Duveiller 2018; Lejeune 2020). Both regressions are local (a moving window of 5 by 5 cells, one regression per window and per month), ordinary least squares, and linear on the albedo scale; the pure-class albedos are read off by predicting at 100 % cover. The other two papers do not regress on fractions: Li 2015 differences nearly pure forest and open pixels inside small windows; Boisier 2013 takes class albedos from nearly pure pixels and mixes them linearly.
3. **Snow.** Lejeune fits separate regressions for snow-free and snow-covered cells and does not recombine them. Boisier derives separate snow-free and snow-covered class albedos and recombines them with the snow-cover fraction (equation 1). Duveiller and Li do not separate snow states at all.
4. **Reliability rules are local co-occurrence rules.** Three of the four papers keep an estimate only where both classes are present near each other: a co-occurrence index of at least 0.5 (Duveiller), minimum counts and minimum fractions (Lejeune: 15 of 25 cells; each class at least 10 % of the cell), both classes present in the window (Li). Boisier instead requires pixels more than 95 % pure and fills the gaps by interpolation, which the paper names as a source of error.
5. **Closest relative of the project's diagnostic (my assessment):** for the estimation, Lejeune 2020, with Duveiller 2018 as its parent method; for the quantity (a partial exchange between two classes with the third class and snow held fixed), Boisier's "normalised" albedo change (equation 2). The project differs chiefly in using one continental fit with a smooth of location in place of local windows, a logit link in place of a straight line on the albedo scale, and a 10-point exchange in place of a complete transition.
6. **An observation-based estimate from one-degree cells:** one degree is too coarse for the neighbourhoods of Duveiller, Li and Boisier (all built from 0.05-degree pixels; Duveiller explicitly rejects 0.25 degrees). It is not too coarse for Lejeune's method, which was built for climate-model cells of about two degrees, but that method has only been tested on model output, with stated errors that in July reach the size of the tree-to-crop contrast in the northeastern United States. Duveiller's own results are published as a one-degree monthly data set with uncertainties, and that is what Lejeune set beside the models.

## 2. What each paper does

### 2.1 Lejeune et al. (2020), Earth System Dynamics 11, 1209-1232

Read in full (main text), plus Table S1, Table S2 and the figure captions of the supplement.

| Question | What the paper does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | Two quantities. (a) The "potential" surface albedo change for a transition from trees to crops-grasses under present-day conditions: the albedo of crops-grasses minus the albedo of trees, each being the albedo a cell would have at 100 % of that class. Plain albedo units; figures show it multiplied by 100. (b) The albedo change caused by historical conversion, equal to the fraction converted times a regression slope. | Sect. 2.3.1, p. 1213 (eqs 3-6 and the paragraph after); Sect. 2.3.2, eqs 7-8, pp. 1213-1214; captions of Figs 4, 10, 11 |
| **Class pairs** | Only trees versus crops-grasses (crops, grasses and pastures merged). Shrub fraction is a predictor, but shrub albedo is not reconstructed, for its "limited spatial occurrence". Observational "trees" are the forest classes (taller than 5 m); "crops-grasses" are croplands and grassland; mosaic classes are left out. | Sect. 2.2, p. 1212; Sect. 2.3.1, p. 1213; Sect. 2.1.1, p. 1211; supplement Table S1 |
| **Estimation** | For every land cell, a window of 5 by 5 model cells centred on it (a "big box", up to 25 values), on the model's original grid. Within the box, per month, a multiple linear regression of cell albedo on the fractions of trees, shrubs and crops-grasses, with an intercept: albedo = b0 + b1 x trees + b2 x shrubs + b3 x crops-grasses (fractions in per cent). Pure-class albedo at the central cell = b0 + b1 x 100 % (trees) and b0 + b3 x 100 % (crops-grasses), described as extrapolating the "partial linear regression lines". Linear on the albedo scale. Local, not global. | Sect. 2.3.1, p. 1213, eqs 1-6; Fig. 1, p. 1214 |
| **Sum to one** | Not discussed. The three fractions need not sum to one because other cover exists; the rule is that the included predictors sum to more than 90 % in at least 15 cells. | p. 1213 |
| **Elevation, climate** | The present-day regression has no elevation, climate or location terms; background climate is held fixed only by the box. The historical-change regression does add them as straight-line predictors: albedo change = g0 + g1 x conversion rate + g2 x latitude + g3 x longitude + g4 x elevation, per box and month. Cells with less than 50 % land are excluded. | eqs 1-2 versus eq. 7, p. 1213; Fig. 1 |
| **Time** | One regression per box and per month, on a monthly climatology (2000-2004 for the models). Results are shown for January and July only. | pp. 1212-1213; Sect. 4; p. 1228 |
| **Snow** | Separated. If the central cell has a snow-cover fraction below 0.1 it counts as snow-free and the regression uses only snow-free cells of the box; if above 0.9 it counts as snow-covered and uses only snow-covered cells. Cells in between are dropped. The two are never recombined. | Sect. 2.3.1, p. 1213; p. 1216 |
| **Reliability rules** | (1) A class enters the regression only if its fraction is above zero in at least two cells of the right snow state. (2) The box must hold at least 15 cells of that snow state in which the included fractions sum to more than 90 %. (3) Albedos below 0 or above 1 are removed. (4) For single-class albedos: discard cells where the standard error of the regression exceeds 0.01, or where trees plus crops-grasses cover less than 20 %. (5) For the transition difference: discard cells where trees or crops-grasses cover less than 10 % "and" where the standard error exceeds 0.001 (wording as printed). (6) Historical regression: at least 15 land cells in the box. | p. 1213; Fig. 1 |
| **Uncertainty** | Present-day method: no error bar per estimate; instead the method is tested on a model that also outputs the albedo of each class inside a cell (the Community Land Model version 4.5 at 1.9 by 2.5 degrees). Root-mean-square errors, July: 0.0085 trees, 0.0097 crops-grasses, 0.0189 for the difference. January: 0.019 and 0.013 globally, 0.037 and 0.0295 north of 40 N; for the difference 0.025 globally and 0.0505 north of 40 N. Historical method: the regression is repeated for each ensemble member and with each cell of the box left out in turn (jackknife); the median is kept. | Sect. 3, pp. 1215-1217; Sect. 2.3.2, p. 1214 |
| **Aggregation and presentation** | Maps: the observation-based map in the top-left panel, then one panel per model, same colour scale, for July and January (class albedos Figs 6-9; transition Figs 10-11). Differences between model and observation are called meaningful when they exceed the method's own error. Area-weighted averaging is used to bring 0.05-degree class albedos to two degrees. Multi-model means down-weight models that share a land scheme. | Sect. 4, pp. 1217-1224; Sect. 2.1.1, p. 1211; Fig. 12 caption |
| **Observation side** | Class albedos: 0.05-degree cells at least 95 % one class (GlobCover land cover, GlobAlbedo, mean of white-sky and black-sky), monthly climatology, area-weighted to two degrees. Transition: the one-degree data set of Duveiller et al. (the four-class version), not the box regression. The box regression is applied to models only. | Sects 2.1.1-2.1.2, pp. 1211-1212 |
| **Stated limits** | The error depends on season and is larger with snow. Coverage is lost in snowy months because partly snow-covered cells are dropped; the paper gives western Europe and the northeastern United States in January as examples. The error of the difference "can attain similar magnitudes" to the tree-to-crop contrast in western Europe and the northeastern United States. A class "albedo" mixes canopy and soil. Only January and July were analysed. The method assumes that differences between neighbouring cells in a box stand in for a change of cover. | pp. 1211, 1216, 1217, 1227, 1228 |

### 2.2 Duveiller, Hooker and Cescatti (2018), Nature Communications 9, 679, with its data descriptor (Scientific Data 5, 180014)

Article read in full; descriptor read in full as text; descriptor addendum read.

| Question | What the paper does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | The "potential" change in a surface variable for a "complete transition" from one vegetation class to another: change from A to B = value predicted for pure B minus value predicted for pure A. Reverse transitions have the opposite sign. For albedo the unit is plain albedo; the article's figures show it as reflected shortwave radiation in watts per square metre (albedo change times incoming shortwave), while the data set carries the albedo change itself. | p. 2; eq. 11, p. 9; Fig. 3 caption, p. 4; p. 10; descriptor Table 3 and "Data Records" |
| **Class pairs** | 45 transitions among ten classes (evergreen broadleaf, deciduous broadleaf, evergreen needleleaf, deciduous needleleaf and mixed forests; savannas; shrublands; grasslands; croplands; wetlands) and 6 transitions among four broad classes (forests, shrublands, crops and grasses, savannas). Includes deciduous broadleaf to evergreen needleleaf forest. | descriptor Table 1 and "Retrieving the local biophysical signal"; article pp. 2-5 |
| **Estimation** | Moving window of 5 by 5 pixels at 0.05 degrees (25 pixels, about 25 km), over which the local climate is "assumed to be uniform". In each window, ordinary least squares of the 25 albedo values on the cover fractions, linear on the albedo scale, with an intercept. All classes are in the regression (the vegetation classes plus urban, water, snow/ice and bare soil); predictions are made only for vegetation classes. Local, not global. | "Retrieving the local biophysical signal", p. 9, eqs 3-10; descriptor, same heading |
| **Sum to one** | Handled explicitly. The paper says fractions that sum to one cannot be used directly (three columns hold only two dimensions; risk of spurious correlations). The fraction matrix is centred on its column means and decomposed (singular value decomposition, close to principal components); the dimensions holding 100 % of the variation are kept, which drops the redundant one; albedo is regressed on these. The albedo of a pure class is the prediction for a "dummy pixel" with that class at one and the rest at zero, passed through the same transformation. | p. 9, eqs 5-10 |
| **Elevation, climate** | No covariates. Climate is controlled by the small window; relief by masking, with three conditions on elevation computed from fine elevation data: average within-pixel standard deviation across the window below 50 m; mean elevation of the central pixel within 100 m of the window mean; standard deviation in the central pixel within 100 m of the window value. | p. 10; descriptor "Masking out high topographical variability", eqs 17-19 |
| **Time** | Each of the 12 months separately, on a monthly climatology (the median of each month over 2008-2012). Albedo is the mean of black-sky and white-sky from the product MCD43C3, taking the 16-day period closest to the 15th of the month. | pp. 8-9 |
| **Snow** | Not separated. Snow enters only through the monthly albedo as observed. Neither the article nor the descriptor separates snow-covered from snow-free conditions (I searched both for every mention of snow). | whole text |
| **Reliability rules** | (1) All 25 pixels of the window must have data. (2) At least 10 pixels with different compositions. (3) A co-occurrence index for the pair of classes must be at least 0.5: place evenly spaced ideal points along the line where the two classes alone make up the pixel; for each, take the distance to the nearest real pixel; index = 1 minus (sum of these distances) divided by (sum of the distances from the ideal points to "neither class present"). It runs from 0 (neither class) to 1 (both fully and evenly present). (4) The three relief conditions. (5) After aggregation to one degree, at least 20 of the possible 400 fine estimates. (6) Removal of statistical outliers (threshold not given). | p. 9; pp. 9-10; descriptor "Masking out low vegetation co-occurrences", eqs 14-16 |
| **Uncertainty** | Per window, the standard deviation of the difference from the covariance matrix of the regression coefficients: variance of A plus variance of B minus twice their covariance, with the covariance matrix of the pure-class predictions obtained from the coefficient covariance (eqs 12-13). The paper stresses that the covariance term matters because both predictions come from one regression. Aggregation to one degree: weights from the inverse variances, adjusted for the overlap between neighbouring windows through a matrix of overlap fractions; the aggregated variance is 1 divided by the sum of all elements of the inverse of the resulting covariance matrix (eqs 14-19). | p. 9, eqs 12-13; p. 10, eqs 14-19 |
| **Aggregation and presentation** | Fine estimates are averaged to one degree with the weights above, partly because one degree is easier to compare with land surface models. Each one-degree value comes with its standard deviation and its number of fine samples. Global summaries are a mean with plus or minus two standard errors (Fig. 3); other summaries are by temperature and precipitation bins (Fig. 4) and by low, middle and high latitudes (Fig. 6). Area weighting of the global means is not stated. | p. 10; Fig. 3 caption p. 4; Fig. 6 caption p. 6; descriptor "Data Records" |
| **Stated limits** | Local direct effects only: climate feedbacks and distant effects "cannot be assessed". Vegetation is assumed to be the only driver inside the window; soil differences are ignored. Uncertainty of the input data is not included. No adequate ground network exists for validation. Values exist only where the two classes co-occur on flat enough ground. The 2008-2012 values may not hold under a changed climate, for example where spring snow is lost. A full change over a whole one-degree cell would differ. | pp. 7, 9, 10, 11; descriptor "Usage Notes" |

### 2.5 Related sources that these papers point to

- **The regression-on-fractions method originates in Duveiller et al. (2018).** The article calls it a "novel methodology" (abstract) that needs no fixed "thresholds in cover fractions" (p. 7). Lejeune calls its own box regression a "new methodology" for model output, "similar to" methods applied to satellite data (p. 1210; pp. 1212-1213). There is no earlier methods paper to chase; the data descriptor is the technical companion and is summarised in section 2.2.
- **Addendum to the descriptor (Scientific Data, 2021, doi 10.1038/s41597-020-00787-6).** A second version of the data set, hosted in the Joint Research Centre data catalogue, adds a variable with the suffix "_ext" that extends each transition to places where only one of the two classes occurs, using a random-forest prediction from local climate (including a snow-presence predictor). The original values remain in each file. Lejeune used such a gap-filled version for the forcing calculation (p. 1212).
- **Duveiller et al. (2018), Earth System Science Data 10, 1265-1279** (read: sections 1 to 2.4). A framework for setting land surface models beside the satellite data set. Each model is run with every cell wholly covered by one plant type; the difference between two runs is the model's transition. Model values are kept only where the satellite estimate exists (Sect. 2.3). Comparison is in three views: maps of annual means, latitude bands by month, and temperature-precipitation space (Sect. 2.4). Agreement is measured with a symmetric index, the correlation and the mean absolute bias (eqs 1-4).
- **Public code for Lejeune's method.** The recipe "recipe_albedolandcover.yml" and script "landcover/albedolandcover.py" in the ESMValTool repository (read online, not run). The recipe sets the box at 5 by 5, the minimum predictor sum at 90, the minimum cells per class at 2 and the minimum cells per box at 15, and regrids models to two degrees first. The script computes each pure-class albedo as intercept plus coefficient times 100 and names its output "albedo_change_from_tree_to_grass-crop-pasture", unit 1. The recipe is marked unmaintained and differs from the paper in small details, so the paper is the reference.

### 2.8 Points the papers leave unstated or ambiguous

- Lejeune's filter for the transition difference joins its two conditions with "and" (p. 1213). Whether a cell is dropped when either holds or only when both hold is not clear, and the threshold on the difference (0.001) is ten times tighter than for single classes (0.01). Reported as printed.
- Lejeune does not say how the regression behaves when the three fractions sum to exactly 100 % in every cell of a box.
- Duveiller's first relief condition is worded differently in the article (standard deviation of elevation within the window, p. 10) and in the descriptor (the average of within-pixel standard deviations). The outlier threshold is not given. Quality filtering of the albedo input is not described in the article or descriptor; only Lejeune mentions it (p. 1211).
- Li gives the elevation correction for temperature only.
- Li, Boisier and Duveiller (Fig. 3) do not say whether their regional or global means are area-weighted.

## 3. Assessment (mine, not the papers')

To be sure of the project's side I read the design check of the diagnostic at `(session scratchpad, not kept) 2026-11-10_cover_contrast_test_design_check_codex_gpt6astra.md`. Two of the candidate changes below touch points where that check advised otherwise; I mark them.

### 3.1 The closest published relative

**For the estimation: Lejeune et al. (2020).** It is the only paper of the four that regresses the albedo of coarse cells with mixed cover on their cover fractions, which is what the project does. It also works by month, treats snow-free and snow-covered conditions separately, uses three cover classes, uses area-weighted averages, and exists in order to set model results beside satellite-based ones. Its second regression (equation 7) puts latitude, longitude and elevation beside the cover term, which is the straight-line counterpart of the project's smooth of location plus elevation. Duveiller et al. (2018) is the parent method and is closer on three technical points: fractions that sum to one, a standard error from the coefficient covariance that keeps the covariance between the two predictions (the project's calculation is of this kind), and transitions between forest types.

**For the quantity: Boisier's normalised albedo change (equation 2).** It is the only published quantity here that starts from a partial change in cover between two groups with the third group and the snow held constant, as the project's exchange does. Boisier divides by the fraction changed and reads the result as the change for complete conversion.

**Where the project differs from all of them:**

1. **One continental fit, not local fits.** The published regressions are refitted in every window, so the class contrast is free to differ from place to place, and "same background climate" means "same small window". The project fits one model per month to all cells and holds background fixed through the smooth of location and elevation. A regional mean in the project is therefore the continental cover response evaluated at that region's cells, not a contrast estimated from that region's data. No paper here does this; Lejeune's equation 7 is the nearest.
2. **Logit link, not a straight line on the albedo scale.** In the papers the contrast within a window does not depend on the starting mix, and prediction at 100 % cover is the stated procedure. In the project the response is curved, and the diagnostic evaluates it at each cell's own mix.
3. **A 10-point exchange, not a complete transition.** No paper reports a centred partial exchange.
4. **A continental range rule, not a local co-occurrence rule.** The papers ask whether both classes occur near each other. The project asks whether the two endpoints lie inside the range of mixes seen anywhere in the training data.
5. **Classes.** The project puts shrubs with trees by leaf habit. Boisier counts shrubs as grasses; Duveiller and Lejeune keep shrubs separate; Li leaves them out. The published open class is crops and grasses only, with bare ground separate.
6. **Snow definition.** The project splits by the snow flag of each retrieval and so has an albedo for each snow state. This resembles Boisier, who builds separate snow-free and snow-covered albedo climatologies from the satellite product's own snow information (the paper does not detail how). Lejeune splits by the cell's monthly snow-cover fraction because models give only a mixed cell albedo. The project's all-days fit corresponds to Duveiller and Li.

### 3.3 An observation-based estimate from the project's own one-degree data

**Is one degree too coarse? It depends on the paper.**

| Method | Neighbourhood it needs | One-degree cells |
|---|---|---|
| Duveiller | 25 pixels of 0.05 degrees in about 25 km, climate assumed uniform | Too coarse. The authors would not use a 0.25-degree product because it is "too coarse" to keep climate conditions homogeneous in the window (p. 10). The relief limits (50 m, 100 m) cannot be met either. |
| Li | Pixels of 0.05 degrees more than 80 % one class, within about 50 by 28 km | Too coarse. |
| Boisier | Pixels of 0.05 degrees more than 95 % one class | Needs the fine pixels as input; one degree is fine only as the output grid. |
| Lejeune | 5 by 5 cells of a climate model; two degrees is called about the model resolution (p. 1211); tested at 1.9 by 2.5 degrees (p. 1215) | Not too coarse. One degree is finer than the cells it was built for. |

**What a local regression on the project's cells would take, following Lejeune with Duveiller's refinements.** Per cell and month: observed albedo for the snow state, the three fractions, and the cell area. For each cell, its 5 by 5 block; at least 15 usable cells; each class present in at least two; least squares on the albedo scale; the sum-to-one redundancy handled as in Duveiller's equations 5 to 10; pure-class predictions and their difference; a standard deviation with the covariance term (equations 12-13); the filters of section 2.1; then regional means that allow for the overlap between neighbouring blocks (Duveiller's equations 14-19). The published script is a few hundred lines. I have not designed it further.

**What the papers imply about how far to trust it:**

1. No paper applies the coarse-cell regression to observed albedo. Lejeune applies it to models only and takes the observation side from 0.05-degree data (Sects 2.1.1-2.1.2). The project would be the first use on observations at this scale.
2. Its only test is against a model whose class albedos are known. The stated error of the tree-to-crop difference is about 0.02 in July and about 0.05 north of 40 N in January (pp. 1216-1217). The paper names the northeastern United States as a place where the error reaches the size of the contrast (p. 1217) and where January coverage is lost (p. 1216).
3. My inference from numbers in two papers (Lejeune's error refers to a different pair of classes and to a model test, so this is indicative only). The evergreen-to-deciduous contrast in Boisier's Table 2 is 0.049 in snow-free summer, 0.023 in snow-free winter and 0.039 with snow. Against Lejeune's stated errors that is about two and a half times the July error (0.019), about equal to it in snow-free winter, and smaller than the error with snow (0.05). The tree-to-open contrast with snow (about 0.3) is six times that error. A coarse-cell regression would therefore be expected to resolve tree-to-open contrasts with snow clearly, summer contrasts moderately, and to struggle with the leaf-habit contrast in winter.
4. The method rests on neighbouring cells sharing a background climate. Within a block five degrees across, the evergreen and summergreen fractions vary with latitude and elevation, so the leaf-habit contrast is the one most exposed to that assumption. Lejeune adds latitude, longitude and elevation terms in the historical regression; the present-day regression has none.

**The route the papers themselves took.** Lejeune (Sect. 2.1.2) and the 2018 framework paper set models beside Duveiller's published one-degree monthly data set. It holds the albedo change, its standard deviation and its sample count for 45 transitions, among them deciduous broadleaf to evergreen needleleaf forest and each forest type to grassland or cropland. Its limits for the project: all-conditions albedo only (no snow split); classes that keep shrubs, crops and grasses apart; the 2008-2012 climatology; values only where the two classes co-occur at fine scale on flat ground, so coverage over North America has to be checked from the sample counts. It is a complete-transition quantity, so the scale question of changes 2a to 2c applies. The article gives a figshare address for the first version (p. 11); the addendum gives the Joint Research Centre catalogue for the second.

**The only published route to an observation-based estimate split by snow state** is the pure-pixel one (Boisier Sect. 2.1; Lejeune Sect. 2.1.1). It needs the 0.05-degree albedo and fine land cover, not the one-degree table.

### 3.4 Questions that arise for Chris (undecided)

1. Should the project's number be shown per 10 points of cover, with published values multiplied by 0.10 (change 2b), or per unit of cover fraction in Boisier's manner (change 2a)? The first agrees with the earlier design check; the second has a direct field precedent but leans on a straight-line reading of a curved model. Change 2c, the literal published definition, is listed for completeness.
2. Should a local co-occurrence rule from the papers (change 6) be added to, or replace, the continental range rule? The earlier design check judged a local-support filter to be more than a small addition.
3. Should the recombined all-conditions contrast (change 5) be added beside the fitted all-days result?
4. Should Duveiller's published one-degree data set be the observation-based comparison? That needs decisions on how its classes map to the project's three, and on which version (original values, or the extended ones).
5. Is a Lejeune-style local regression on the project's own cells wanted, given that the papers suggest it would be informative for tree-to-open contrasts and weak for the leaf-habit contrast in winter?
