| Lower sensitivity | **0.50 in all twelve months** | Exposed-ice-like effective albedo; supported by Bradley’s ablation values |

Applying each value everywhere and throughout the year is **a project choice**. These cases are neither confidence limits nor universal physical bounds: the literature also contains darker margins and brighter dry snow. Their purpose is to show how strongly the paper’s conclusions depend on the assumed effective glacier albedo. The supplied threshold calculation already demonstrates substantial sensitivity, **2.50–6.51 W/m²** across 0.50–0.80 for 8–10 ka; those are not newly calculated results for the partial-ice headline.

Keeping a fixed reference is justified **provisionally by the absence of an appropriate monthly climatology**, not by an absence of real seasonality. I would not replace 0.68 with 0.60 merely because another model uses it: that supplies precedent, not evidence that it is closer to the required average.

**The ice fraction and kernels impose three practical requirements.**

First, retain
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{cell},m}=f\,\alpha_{\mathrm{glacier},m}+(1-f)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{vegetation},m}.
\]
Here glacier albedo includes the snow and firn **on the glacier**. Snow-covered fraction within a glacier is different from glacier-covered fraction within a grid cell. Bern3D provides precedent for the latter mixing; Willeit and Ganopolski distinguish the former.

Second, a whole-sheet mean is not necessarily the appropriate effective value for retreat. With the same monthly glacier value at both dates, its influence on the albedo difference is proportional to \(f_{\mathrm{young}}-f_{\mathrm{old}}\). This is algebra from the pipeline’s equation. It concentrates sensitivity where ice area changes, but does **not** justify assigning bare-ice albedo automatically: land lost over millennia may have been snowy interior at the older date.

Third, use broadband surface albedo under compatible illumination. **Black-sky means direct illumination; white-sky means diffuse illumination; blue-sky combines them.** An all-sky TOA kernel does not convert black- or white-sky surface albedo into blue-sky albedo. Apply each monthly albedo difference to its corresponding monthly kernel, then average the fluxes; multiplying by sunlight or cloud transmission again would duplicate effects already represented by the kernel. The monthly kernel method and its atmospheric-state dependence are described by **Bright and O’Halloran (2019), “Developing a monthly radiative kernel for surface albedo change from satellite climatologies of Earth’s shortwave radiation budget: CACK v1.0,” *Geoscientific Model Development*, 12, 3975–3990**, pp. 3977–3979, DOI [10.5194/gmd-12-3975-2019](https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-12-3975-2019). Keeping modern/preindustrial kernels remains a separate approximation; changing glacier albedo alone does not reconstruct deglacial clouds or insolation.
